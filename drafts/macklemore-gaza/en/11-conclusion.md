# Conclusion

<p class="verse">﴿قُلْ هَلْ يَسْتَوِي الَّذِينَ يَعْلَمُونَ وَالَّذِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ ۗ إِنَّمَا يَتَذَكَّرُ أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ﴾</p>

*"Say: Are those who know equal to those who do not know? Only people of understanding take heed."*
*— Surah Az-Zumar 39:9 (part)*

This book began with an image on a phone screen, and it ends with a question about knowledge and responsibility. The verse distinguishes those who know from those who do not, and makes reflection the fruit of a living mind. In these pages I have tried to move the reader from impression to documented knowledge.

We saw how Benjamin Haggerty grew up in Seattle and rose independently to the top of American music, and how in November 2023 he chose to stand on a stage in Washington, acknowledging the limits of his knowledge while declaring what he saw as true. We saw how Hind Rajab's name became a song that reached millions and whose proceeds went to refugees, and how he erred in Seattle and admitted it without abandoning his position.

Then came the two nights at MetLife, where he told ninety thousand people that every human being deserves the same freedom — and the price was removal from his friend's tour. We set out the critics' arguments as they made them: Pink's fear, the Israeli American Council's objections, Robert Kraft's statement, and Ed Sheeran's explanation. And we saw other artists withdraw in solidarity, turning an attempt at silencing into a revealing moment.

My conclusions are these:

1. An honest word still carries a price in even the most open democracies, and the price rises as the platform grows.

2. Fairness toward opponents does not weaken a just cause; it strengthens it.

3. Precision of language is part of integrity, and a single slip can be used to discredit years of principled stance.

4. Human solidarity across faiths and ethnicities is possible, and peoples who have known injustice are closest to understanding it.

5. Loyalty to those who stand with the oppressed is a duty — but it must never become veneration.

### A Personal Reflection

When I think about this story, I return to Hind's voice calling out. A child who never knew Macklemore, never knew Ed Sheeran, never heard of MetLife Stadium. Yet her name reached that stage. To me, that is the meaning of the sentence this book began with: what mattered was not the number of shows, but what was said in them, and who was remembered.

I write these lines from Michigan, in a country whose space for freedom has allowed me to research, write, and publish. That is precisely why it pains me to see that space narrow when the subject is Palestine. I hope this book is a small contribution to keeping it open — through words that are documented and fair.

I ask God to have mercy on Hind Rajab and every innocent life lost, to lift injustice from the oppressed everywhere, and to make our words a testimony of truth, never of falsehood.

**And God knows best.**
