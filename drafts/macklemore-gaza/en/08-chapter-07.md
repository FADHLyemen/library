# Chapter Seven: The Other Side, Fairly Stated

<p class="verse">﴿وَلَا يَجْرِمَنَّكُمْ شَنَآنُ قَوْمٍ عَلَىٰ أَلَّا تَعْدِلُوا ۚ اعْدِلُوا هُوَ أَقْرَبُ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ﴾</p>

*"Do not let hatred of a people lead you to be unjust. Be just — that is nearer to God-consciousness."*
*— Surah Al-Ma'idah 5:8 (part)*

This is among the Quran's greatest verses on justice: do not let hostility toward a people lead you to abandon justice toward them. Its plain meaning is that disagreement, however intense, never permits distorting an opponent's position or ignoring their argument. That is why this chapter presents what the other side said, in its own terms, before I discuss it.

### Robert Kraft

In a lengthy statement, Kraft confirmed that he had decided to bar Macklemore from the Gillette Stadium shows on September 25 and 26. He said the stadium has a longstanding commitment to a welcoming environment for all guests and to ensuring its events do not provide a platform for hate speech. He based the decision on what he called Macklemore's recent actions, material shared from the stage at the New Jersey shows, and a "broader history" of rhetoric and imagery that he considers antisemitic and hurtful to the Jewish community.

Kraft added that the decision was not about diminishing the suffering of innocent Palestinians or denying anyone the right to advocate for them, and that their pain and loss are real. But he argued that such advocacy should not come at the expense of the Jewish community or obscure the responsibility of Hamas, which the United States and other countries designate a terrorist organization. He said that sharing selective information while ignoring Hamas's actions is not honest, noted that he had for decades directed resources to Palestine and created athletic and entrepreneurial opportunities for Palestinians he respects, and said he would welcome sitting down with Macklemore to discuss the facts, since the goal is ultimately the same: peace for all, an end to suffering, and an end to hate.

Kraft's statement did not address Macklemore's claim that he had rallied other stadium owners.

### Pink and the Israeli American Council

The previous chapter set out Pink's argument — that the fear she and other Jewish audience members felt was real, and that she had asked no one to be silenced — and the Council's argument that a pop concert is no place for a one-sided political agenda.

### Ed Sheeran

On September 15, Sheeran posted his statement. He said he is appalled by the conflict between Israel and Palestine and that the suffering on both sides is a human tragedy. He explained that after the New Jersey shows, venues communicated they would pull the concerts if Macklemore remained, and that he was told this stance was based on how Macklemore conveyed part of his message rather than on everything he said. He said he had spoken at length with the venues all week to try to build a bridge — Robert Kraft among them — and had joined talks with promoters and activists on both sides, but the venues' and promoter's decision was final.

Sheeran stressed that removing Macklemore was the promoter's decision, not his, since Macklemore's contract was with the promoter. He said he would never let down fans who had made plans, nor abandon the crew and musicians who depend on him for their livelihood. He rejected the charge of complicity, saying he has personal views on this devastating conflict, that choosing not to speak publicly does not mean he doesn't care, and that his shows are about unity, joy, and a place where everyone feels welcome.

As noted earlier, Israel rejects the genocide characterization and maintains that its campaign is self-defense following the attack of October 7, 2023.

### A Fair Discussion

Having set out these arguments, here are my observations as a reader trying to weigh them:

1. **The fear is real, but the measure is the words.** I don't doubt that Pink and others felt afraid; feelings are not to be dismissed. But the fair question is whether the words themselves were incitement. What Macklemore said at MetLife was a call for freedom and dignity for every human being, and contained no call to harm anyone. As singer Shirley Manson observed, nothing he said was violent or hateful.

2. **"One-sided" is not an indictment of an artist.** Artists are not asked to be balanced news networks. Protest songs throughout history have sided with the oppressed as their authors saw them. Demands for balance become selective when imposed on one voice and not others.

3. **But the Seattle slip is a legitimate criticism.** Fairness requires acknowledging that the chant was a mistake, as Macklemore himself admitted. Critics are entitled to raise it — provided it is not used to dismiss everything he has said since.

4. **Owners' rights and artistic freedom.** A stadium owner has rights over his venue; that is understood in the American system. But if, as Macklemore says, several owners joined in a collective ultimatum over two words, the matter goes beyond managing a facility to controlling what may be said on the country's biggest stages.

5. **Criticizing a state is not hating a people.** This is the heart of the whole dispute. Criticizing a government's policies, or describing its actions as a UN commission has described them, is not hatred of Jews. I discuss this at length in my book *Legitimate Criticism and the Boundaries of Antisemitism*. We too must guard this distinction carefully and never let our own speech slide from criticizing an occupation into insulting the followers of a faith.

By this measure, the critics' arguments deserve a hearing, and some carry sincere concerns. But they are not enough to justify removing an artist for calling for freedom for a besieged people.
